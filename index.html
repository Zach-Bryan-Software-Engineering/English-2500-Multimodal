The argument

Viewers should consult more than one review before choosing a show, because critics and audiences often judge the same series differently, and only by weighing both can you find out which opinion matches your own taste.

Review sites feel like a shortcut: one number, one decision. But a single number hides who gave it and what they were judging. This site puts critic and audience scores side by side so you can see how often they disagree, and what to do when they do.

The evidence

Same show. Different verdicts.

Scores are Rotten Tomatoes’ Tomatometer (critics) and Popcornmeter (audience): the share of reviews that are positive. Most come from Rotten Tomatoes pages checked on Nov. 16, 2026. A few come from news reports, marked on their cards, and may have changed. Poster images load from free sources (TVmaze and Wikipedia) when you are online.

Note: Not every show splits viewers. Several seasons here, such as Stranger Things Season 1, show critics and audiences nearly agreeing. That is exactly why checking both is useful: when they match, you can be more confident.

The reasons

Why do the scores split?

Different judges

Critics watch whole seasons for work, often before release. Audiences are mostly people who already chose the show, and many are devoted fans of the source material.

Different questions

A critic may ask, “Is this well made?” A fan may ask, “Is this the story I loved?” Both are fair, but they produce different numbers.

Backlash campaigns

Audience scores can be swept up in organized outrage. Coverage of The Last of Us’s second season described it as being review bombed (“Just Like the Game”).

Percent isn’t “stars”

Both meters report the share of reviews that are positive, not how much people loved it. An 84% can mean a lot of lukewarm approvals.

Your game plan

Before you press play

Click each step as you complete it.

  1. Check the critics. Find the critic score and read one or two full reviews, not just the headline number.
  2. Check the audience. Compare it with viewer scores and skim a few audience comments, noticing what they praise or complain about.
  3. Look at the gap. If the scores match, trust them. If they don’t, find out why (pacing? faithfulness to the book or game? a loud backlash?).
  4. Match it to your taste. Decide which kind of reviewer sounds most like you, then watch the pilot and judge for yourself.
